# gardening glove research
- Leather is the usual choice for thorns and digging; goatskin is often named as a good balance of flexibility and strength. Leather is less breathable and less dexterous, soaks through and stiffens when wet.
- Nitrile or latex coated knit gloves give better grip, dexterity and wet-soil performance; sources disagree on how well a thin coating stops thorns, so do not promise thorn protection for coated knit gloves.
- Practical middle ground: a heavy leather pair for thorns and digging, a light coated pair for weeding and planting.
- Fit: snug but not tight. Loose gloves reduce dexterity, tight ones restrict movement. Sizing varies by brand, so check the maker's chart.
- Nitrile can crack from long UV exposure; store out of direct sun. Wash coated gloves per maker directions and air dry.
- EN 388:2016 rates abrasion, blade cut, tear and puncture (plus an A to F sliding-blade cut letter); a rating only helps if the listing states it. Thorns are low-force punctures, so puncture rating and a long cuff matter more than cut rating.
- Gauntlet cuffs of about 40 cm (16 in) protect wrists and forearms from brambles and roses.
- Foam padding on knuckles protects the back of the hand from impact; touchscreen fingertips are a convenience only.
