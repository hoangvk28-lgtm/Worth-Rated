# soil moisture meter research
- Analog dial probes are cheap, need no battery, and rarely publish accuracy; treat them as relative guides (this pot reads drier than that one), not absolute scales.
- Digital capacitive meters are generally rated more accurate and durable and resist salinity better; cheap resistance probes react to fertilizer salts and can read falsely wet or dry.
- Insert to root-zone depth (about two-thirds of root depth), not just to the maximum probe length; shallow readings reflect surface evaporation. Garden guidance varies between about three and six inches.
- Take two or three readings across the planting area; single-point readings mislead in uneven soil. Wait for the needle or display to settle. Avoid reading right after rain; give surface water time to soak in.
- Soil type changes what a reading means: peat mix and sandy mix at the same dial value hold different amounts of water. Calibrate by feel: water a pot, let it drain, take a reading.
- Corrosion of the probe is the most common failure; wipe and dry the probe after use. Fertilizer salts skew readings high. Low batteries in digital units look like dry soil.
- pH and light functions on 3-in-1 meters are coarse: light often in bands, pH unverified without a buffer solution; pH meters may misbehave in very acidic or very alkaline soil. Moisture is the most useful function.
- Sources were retailer and blog pages; treat specifics as general practical advice.
